Thesis Question

How do we recognize authenticity and meaning when visual culture becomes endless, frictionless derivatives generated by machines?

Every day, we are bombarded with images; they hit our brains at a thousand miles an hour, a thousand times a day. It can be hard to keep up with the sheer volume of content thrust at us at any given moment.

Images have become hollow, stripped of meaning.

As "Big AI" companies continue their rapid growth and cyclical funding, they reinvest the wealth initially built on ad revenue and the commodification of our data into endless streams of AI-generated images and videos. Each new wave of investment fuels this feedback loop, accelerating the flood of synthetic visuals and further eroding authenticity. Meaning erodes not only because the volume of images multiplies, but because the systems that generate and circulate them prioritize scale and profit over fact. As these systems produce infinite streams of synthetic content, they not only erode the authenticity of what we see but also transfer creative power from individuals to algorithms and corporate infrastructures. This shift marks a unique moment in history: one where art, truth, and humanity are increasingly mediated through the machine.

This landscape forces us to question not just what it means to be human, but also what it means to know what is real in a world full of endless derivative replication.

AI Generated Content is created by large language models that work by producing outputs based on millions upon millions of images and pieces of data in training sets. These images might include anything from thousands of photos of bird species to thousands of historical artworks; the list goes on. These models absorb, reinterpret, and remix massive archives of human-made images, or UGC.

The large language models are trained on billions of examples, learning patterns so deeply that their outputs feel new while being structurally dependent on everything that came before them.

This moves the means of production out of the user's hands and into the pockets of modern-day factory owners: tech conglomerates.

AI embodies the extreme version of artistic "stealing": not copying a single artist but internalizing all of them at once, creating derivative media that blurs authorship, originality, and ownership.

Overview

Meltdown is a five-channel interactive installation that generates and sequences AI-diffused images across five ontological categories — Substrate, Machine, Circulation, Representation, and Control — in real time. A MediaPipe body-tracking camera detects viewer movement in the gallery: stillness slows the image sequence; movement accelerates it.

Meltdown is an invitation to consider your position within the systems that shape contemporary visual culture. The viewer is not an audience — they are input, substrate, raw material for AI systems.

Framework

Nick Land's Meltdown gave the project its name: technocapital's runaway acceleration eating through human substrate. Mark Fisher diagnosed the flatline of cultural time. Hito Steyerl's In Defense of the Poor Image articulated how degraded, circulating images carry more truth than high-resolution artifacts. Zuboff traced surveillance capitalism's extraction of human experience as raw material. Each pointed to the same rupture: the moment digital systems stop serving human needs and start metabolizing them.

Plato's Cave structures Meltdown's organization. Each of the five thematic folders maps onto a layer of the allegory, beginning with the material substrate (the cave wall itself, the screen), moving through the apparatus that produces images (the machine, the fire), tracking how those images circulate and multiply (the circulation of shadows), examining what gets represented and how we mistake representation for reality, and finally confronting the systems of control that determine which images appear and who gets to look. The folders aren't arbitrary categories, they're stages in a process of gradual disillusionment, where each layer reveals another mechanism by which our experience online is constructed, mediated, and captured. The cave's prisoners watch shadows; we watch feeds. The structure is the same, just iterated through surveillance capitalism's infrastructure.

Category System

The five folders aren't arbitrary categories — they're stages in a process of gradual disillusionment, where each layer reveals another mechanism by which experience online is constructed, mediated, and captured.

01 Substrate

War, climate disaster, natural environments — the physical world and its consequences.

02 Machine

AI, algorithms, data centers, robots, sci-fi imagery, cyber systems — internet infrastructure and tech imagery.

03 Circulation

Feeds, memes, internet culture, virality, aesthetics, trends. The residue. Social media loops.

04 Representation

Deepfakes, AI-generated images, bootlegs, poor images, dataset images. Hyperreality and copies.

05 Control

The people, systems, and governance that control these conditions.

The Installation Experience

Custom Python pipeline using Stable Diffusion — generates fluid AI image transitions, frame by frame.

The code was written with the intention of creating a seemingly endless stream of media representative of modern online media consumption. Riley and I developed a pipeline that takes one image and creates a sequence of generations in transition to another image. We utilized the Stable Diffusion Turbo model to create PNG sequences in order to preserve 16,000 image generations. The code then decodes them and plays them in a sequence based on the behavior of the viewer, whose motion data is being captured by MediaPipe, a motion tracking software. This was done to create an active feedback loop between the user and the media, where both control each other in an endless loop.

When you stand in front of Meltdown, the system doesn't show you images. It shows you being imaged.

Your body becomes input. Motion triggers category switches across the five folders. The faster you move, the more the sequence churns through substrate, machine, circulation, representation, control.

The installation makes explicit what platforms do constantly: convert presence into data, metabolize attention into content, extract gestures as raw material for algorithmic production. You recognize yourself not as audience but as training data. The feedback loop isn't hidden in server farms — it's here, immediate, visible. You are not viewing the system. You are inside it.
